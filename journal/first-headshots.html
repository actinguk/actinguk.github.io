Where should you get your first professional acting headshots in London?

— Begin with a photograph that looks like you, not a glamorous version of someone else. A strong headshot should make casting want to see the actor behind it.

Acting UK editorialPublished 13 November 2026

If you are applying to drama school or just experimenting with acting, check whether a clear natural-light photo is sufficient. When you are ready to approach professional agents or casting, a dedicated actor-headshot session can be a sensible investment. Look for a portfolio showing different ages, faces and casting styles, not just one fashionable lighting setup.

Our recommendation: Kirill Kozlov Photography, a North London actor-headshot specialist whose website describes photographing actors since 2006. His portfolio demonstrates both simple casting portraits and contrasting, more dramatic looks. If you want a session that explores several sides of your casting range while keeping your appearance recognisable, we recommend looking at his work and comparing the available sessions.

Before booking, check the full price, number of retouched photographs, turnaround, usage terms and cancellation policy. Ask what clothing to bring and whether the session suits your current career stage. Compare portfolios and budgets; a good headshot is one part of a casting package, not a promise of auditions.

Your acting headshot session: questions & answers

From choosing a photographer to selecting the final pictures, here are the questions actors bring to headshot sessions. We’ve drawn on UK casting guidance and common questions actors ask before, during and after their sessions. The advice focuses on what matters when choosing a photographer and preparing your professional materials.

Choosing a photographer & booking

Do you need professional headshots if you’re just starting out?

Not necessarily for your first class, amateur production or drama-school application. Follow the application instructions; a clear recent photograph may be enough. When you are ready for professional submissions, an actor-specific session is worth considering because the photographer can help with lighting, expression and a coherent set of casting images. Spotlight’s application guidance calls for a professional headshot. Don’t postpone practising, networking or suitable open auditions while you save up. Decide what you actually need your pictures to do.

Further reading: Spotlight – casting directors’ headshot advice · Spotlight – profile advice for self-represented actors

How do you choose the right headshot photographer?

Start with the individual photographer’s actor portfolio rather than follower counts or celebrity clients. Look for genuine variety across faces, ages, skin tones and expressions. At thumbnail size, can you still see the person clearly? Ask how the photographer directs someone who feels awkward, whether they show you pictures during the shoot, and what they do to achieve different looks without turning you into a character in costume. Get recommendations from your agent or actor friends, then compare work that matches your goals. Years of relevant experience can be reassuring, but judge the recent portfolio and the actual session too.

Further reading: Spotlight – preparing for a headshot session

Should you book a trendy headshot studio or an individual photographer?

Booking a trendy headshot studio can be hit and miss. Some studios work with several photographers under one brand, so the images you like in the portfolio may not be taken by the person who photographs you on the day.

We would recommend choosing an experienced individual actor-headshot photographer whose work you genuinely like. You know who you are booking, can discuss your ideas directly and have a clearer idea of the results to expect. There’s no need to pay extra just for a fashionable studio name. Compare the photographer’s experience, their own portfolio and what the session includes, rather than the brand.

There’s also the question of value for money. When you book through a larger studio, part of the price may go towards management, marketing, administration and other overheads. When you book an independent photographer directly, you’re paying their business for their time, experience and work. With fewer layers of overhead, an independent photographer may offer a more competitive price for a comparable session.

How much should you budget for acting headshots in London?

There is no single correct price. Compare the total cost, including session length, edited images, extra retouches and any optional services. As a guide, our featured photographer’s Express session is £180 and includes two retouched images; the £280 Spotlight session includes six retouched images and up to two hours in the studio. Check current prices and package details before booking. A shorter session may suit a refresh, while a first portfolio can benefit from more time to explore different looks. Pay for the work you need, not a fashionable brand name.

Further reading: Kirill Kozlov – session prices and inclusions

How long should a headshot session last?

Think about what has to happen in the time you buy. A short session can work if you need a couple of fresh pictures and already know your strongest looks. A longer session can give a first-time actor time to relax, try clothes, change hair and explore subtle expressions. Ask whether changing clothes, reviewing pictures and makeup are included in the booked time. If you want to shave or rest between looks, mention it beforehand. More time is useful only if it helps you get better photographs; hundreds of exposures are not the same as a strong set of final images.

Further reading: Kirill Kozlov – session prices and inclusions

What should you check before paying a deposit?

Get written confirmation of the date, named photographer, session duration, number of edited photographs, total price, payment schedule and cancellation or rescheduling terms. Check how long the proofing gallery remains available, what happens if the photographer is unavailable and whether there is a retouching revision or reshoot policy. If you have accessibility needs, allergies, an under-18 participant or want to bring someone, discuss those arrangements first. Booking conditions differ; don’t assume an audition clash automatically entitles you to a free change. Pay through the photographer’s official booking channel.

What to bring & how to prepare

What should you wear to an acting headshot session?

Bring a small selection of clean, well-fitting tops that you actually feel comfortable wearing. Plain or quietly textured fabrics usually keep attention on the face; avoid large logos and loud patterns. Bring a few different necklines, tones and layers, for example a casual T-shirt, a simple shirt and a darker or more structured layer. Colour is personal: black is not automatically wrong and a bright colour is not automatically right. Try what complements your skin, eyes and overall appearance. A longer session may let you explore more options, but there is no need to buy a new wardrobe.

Further reading: Spotlight – preparing for a headshot session

Should you wear makeup or hire a professional makeup artist?

Makeup is optional. Aim for how you might realistically appear at a casting on a good day rather than a beauty campaign. If you normally wear makeup, bring your usual products and start with a light finish; you can build it up for a second look if appropriate. A makeup artist can be helpful if you want support or have specific skin, hair or access needs, but is not a requirement for an actor headshot. Ask whether makeup takes time from the photography session and whether its cost is included. Do not feel obliged to purchase it as an add-on.

Further reading: Spotlight – preparing for a headshot session

Should you get a haircut or change your hair colour before the shoot?

If you want a new look, give yourself time to settle into it and check that it represents the appearance you will actually maintain for auditions. Avoid an unfamiliar cut, strong colour change or treatment immediately before a shoot unless it is intentional. Bring the products and tools you normally use, and ask whether there is time to try a different parting, tie long hair back or show its natural texture. A variety of hairstyles can help where both are realistic for you; the aim is not to hide your natural hair.

Further reading: Spotlight – preparing for a headshot session · Kirill Kozlov – preparation and booking FAQ

Can you shave halfway through your headshot session?

Yes, if the photographer agrees and there is time and a suitable place to do it. Start with your usual beard or stubble and then shave for a second look, if both appearances are ones you would realistically use. Bring your own shaving kit and allow time for skin to settle. If you have facial hair you intend to keep, do not feel pressured to remove it for casting. Your primary photo should match how you actually look now.

Further reading: Spotlight – casting directors’ headshot advice · Spotlight – preparing for a headshot session

Should you keep your glasses, piercings or jewellery in?

If glasses are part of your everyday appearance, bring them and consider a few photographs both with and without them. A photographer can adjust lighting to reduce reflections. Keep jewellery subtle if it distracts from your face; if piercings or visible tattoos are part of your identity or casting range, discuss which ones you want to show. You can make a second version without removable jewellery if that reflects a look you would genuinely use. Do not remove anything you are uncomfortable removing. The pictures should remain honest.

Further reading: Spotlight – casting directors’ headshot advice

What if you’re nervous and don’t know how to pose?

You do not need to arrive knowing how to model. Tell your photographer that you are nervous and what usually feels difficult in front of a camera. A good session can start with conversation and straightforward direction, gradually exploring small shifts in posture, eyeline, expression and thought. Ask to see a few pictures during the session if that helps. Think about what your character is thinking rather than repeatedly arranging your face. If a pose feels uncomfortable, say so. A photographer who understands actors should be able to adapt without making you feel rushed or self-conscious.

During the shoot

How many different looks should you aim for?

Think of a look as an actual change in impression, not just a different shirt. For example, a relaxed approachable image and a quieter, more focused image may support different casting briefs while still looking like the same person. Try meaningful variations in expression, hair, lighting or clothing. You do not need to dress as a doctor, detective or period character. Spotlight’s recent profile guidance suggests four to six varied photographs; that is a useful aim for a complete professional profile, not a requirement to buy six on your first day. Start with one excellent lead image and build a useful range within budget.

Are studio headshots better than natural-light headshots?

Neither is inherently better. Studio lighting allows the photographer to control light, background and consistency. Natural light can create a gentle, believable portrait but depends on the weather, location and timing. Ask to see examples from the actual photographer in both settings if you are considering them. For casting, prioritise clear eyes, an uncluttered background and an accurate appearance over elaborate lighting. A more dramatic editorial image may complement your main headshot, but should not replace an honest casting image.

Further reading: Spotlight – casting directors’ headshot advice

Should you smile or look serious in your headshots?

There is no single correct expression. Choose a main photograph with a natural, engaged presence that fits your current casting profile. A genuine smile can be useful for warmer or commercial work, while a calmer or more intense expression may suit other opportunities. Try to convey an inner thought rather than force a mood. Avoid six versions of the same expression or exaggerated character faces. Ask your agent what gaps they see if you have representation.

Further reading: Spotlight – casting directors’ headshot advice · Spotlight – profile advice for self-represented actors

What if you wake up with a spot, tired eyes or bad skin?

A temporary blemish is usually not a reason to cancel. Discuss subtle retouching of a spot or stray hair and what can be corrected without changing your appearance. If you are ill or genuinely need rest, contact the photographer and check the rescheduling policy. Don’t try aggressive new skincare, tanning or treatments immediately before your appointment. Your skin texture, permanent features and normal appearance are part of who you are, not things that automatically need removing.

Can you bring a friend, a parent or ask for accessibility adjustments?

Ask in advance about step-free access, accessible facilities, a quiet environment, seating, breaks, communication preferences or the presence of a support person. A good shoot should accommodate reasonable needs without pressuring you to explain more than necessary. Policies on guests vary. For an under-18 performer, confirm the provider’s safeguarding arrangements and parent or guardian attendance rules before booking. If you feel unsafe or uncomfortable during a shoot, you can stop and discuss the issue or leave.

After the session & choosing your photographs

How do you choose the best headshots from hundreds of images?

First discard anything that is out of focus, awkward or no longer looks like you. Review the remaining pictures at thumbnail size and look for clear eyes, a convincing expression and a useful variety. Don’t automatically choose the one you find most flattering; ask your agent or a trusted actor colleague which images best reflect you and the work you pursue. If the photographer offers selection advice, use it. A focused group of four to six useful images is generally stronger than a huge gallery of near-duplicates. Keep your most representative head-and-shoulders image as the lead.

Further reading: Spotlight – casting directors’ headshot advice · Spotlight – preparing for a headshot session · Spotlight – profile advice for self-represented actors

How much retouching is too much? Can you use AI instead?

A good retouch can remove a temporary spot, tidy a stray hair or balance colour and exposure while preserving the real face. Avoid reshaping your jaw, changing eye colour, removing normal skin texture or inventing hair and features. A fully AI-generated likeness may be visually impressive but can misrepresent how you actually appear; it also does not replace any requirement for genuine professional photographs. If you use editing tools, keep the result honest and check the platform’s rules. If you would not recognise the person in an audition room, the image has gone too far.

Further reading: Spotlight – casting directors’ headshot advice · Spotlight – photos and showreels FAQ

Do you get all the photographs, or only the final retouched images?

It depends on the package. A proofing gallery lets you review and choose pictures; it may be low-resolution, watermarked or time-limited, and is not necessarily a set of files you can download and use. Ask how many finished images are included, whether you receive high-resolution JPEGs, whether colour and black-and-white versions are included and the price of extra retouches. Some photographers include a downloadable set of unretouched photographs; others charge for an edited full gallery or do not release it. Confirm what “raw” means: original camera RAW files and unretouched JPEGs are not necessarily the same thing. Compare usable deliverables rather than the number of shutter clicks.

Further reading: Kirill Kozlov – session prices and inclusions

Should you use colour or black and white? What about cropping?

For UK professional casting, a clear colour head-and-shoulders portrait is a sensible primary image. Black and white can be useful as an additional option if the brief or platform allows it. Use the actual final files supplied by your photographer rather than screenshots of the proof gallery. Check the current upload requirements for each platform and keep your main photo closely framed enough for a small casting thumbnail. A wider portrait or full-length photograph can be appropriate for specific commercial briefs, but it is not a replacement for the lead headshot.

Further reading: Spotlight – casting directors’ headshot advice · Spotlight – photos and showreels FAQ

Can you use the photographs on Spotlight, your website and social media? Who owns copyright?

In the UK, paying for a commissioned photograph does not normally transfer copyright to you. The photographer or their employer usually retains it unless a contract says otherwise. Ask for a clear licence covering casting profiles, agency submissions, your professional website, social media and any intended publicity use. Check credit requirements and whether further commercial uses, significant alterations or AI training are permitted. Ask about the photographer’s own portfolio use of your pictures and any opt-out arrangements. This is general information; the particular contract determines what is allowed.

Further reading: UK Intellectual Property Office – copyright guidance · Spotlight – photos and showreels FAQ

What if you don’t like the photos after the session?

Start by identifying the problem: are you worried about your expression, styling, technical quality or whether the photographs resemble you? Ask the photographer for a constructive review and share specific examples. Sometimes a different selection or a small edit helps. If the delivered work does not match the agreed service, refer to the booking terms and raise the concern promptly in writing. Do not assume a free reshoot or refund is automatic. Asking about revisions and reshoots before booking, reviewing images during the session and choosing a photographer whose work suits you can reduce surprises.

Further reading: Spotlight – preparing for a headshot session

How often should you get new headshots?

Update when your current photographs stop representing you: a substantial haircut, a changed appearance, a different casting range or credible feedback that your images are no longer doing their job. Spotlight’s guidance suggests reviewing headshots every couple of years and sooner if your appearance changes significantly. You do not need to replace good, accurate pictures annually just because you see a fashionable new style. Keep your professional profile consistent and remove old images that could confuse casting.
