One chair, several worlds: the intelligence of a small set

How a carefully chosen object can carry a play further than elaborate scenery in an intimate theatre.

Acting UK · 19th November 2026

A chair is a chair until a production persuades us otherwise. It becomes a witness stand, a hospital bed, a throne or the only safe place in a collapsing household. Small theatre frequently relies on that act of agreement between stage and audience.

The apparent simplicity can be deceptive. A single object has to make practical sense through many scenes. Its position, weight, sound and relationship to the actors all become part of the language of the performance. The fewer things on stage, the more conspicuous each decision.

Actors are not merely occupants of a design. They help give it meaning. The way a performer approaches a table can make it an obstacle or a refuge. A coat left on a hook can become a promise that someone will return, even when nobody mentions it.

Minimal design is sometimes romanticised as proof of artistic purity. In reality it can reflect budgetary pressure, a deliberate aesthetic or both. The audience should not be asked to admire a lack of resources for its own sake. The question is what the production does with what it has.

A successful transformation is consistent enough to be understood and flexible enough to surprise. If the same chair represents several places, the performers must establish the rules with clarity. Once those rules are shared, the audience can travel astonishing distances without leaving its seat.

We tend to remember the object that became necessary, not the one that looked expensive. In a small theatre, imagination is often the most carefully engineered part of the set.
