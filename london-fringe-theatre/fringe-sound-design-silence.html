The sound of a small stage includes silence

Why intimate theatre needs restraint in sound design as much as invention.

Acting UK · 21st November 2026

A small London theatre can make a whisper feel like an announcement. It can also make an unnecessary sound cue feel like a lorry reversing through the scene. In intimate spaces, the distance between performer and audience changes what sound design needs to accomplish.

Sound can establish a location, mark a passage of time or reveal something a character cannot say. But a cue should earn its place. When music tells spectators exactly what to feel, it may flatten the ambiguity that made the writing interesting.

Silence is not the absence of design. A quiet room contains breathing, movement and the occasional restless coat. The audience becomes aware of those details when the production gives them permission to listen. That attention can create tension without a single additional speaker.

Technical realities still matter. A cue must be audible from the relevant seats, timed reliably and balanced against dialogue. The correct level in an empty rehearsal room may not be the correct level once an audience has arrived.

Performers benefit from understanding where sound changes the rhythm of a scene. A pause before a cue can be active; a pause after it can let new information land. If the actor merely waits for the operator, the machinery becomes visible in the least helpful way.

We are not arguing for austerity as a virtue. A richly designed sound world can transform a small stage. The question is whether each element enlarges the drama or merely reminds us that someone has found another button.
