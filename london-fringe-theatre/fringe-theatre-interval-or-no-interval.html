The interval is a dramatic decision, not a courtesy

What an uninterrupted performance can offer a small theatre, and what it asks of its audience.

Acting UK · 20th November 2026

An interval offers a theatre audience a familiar bargain: a chance to stretch, talk and return for the second half. But a small production does not automatically become better by observing that convention. Sometimes the break is useful. Sometimes it cuts straight through the pulse of the play.

In an intimate fringe venue, the relationship between audience and actors can feel unusually continuous. A story unfolding in real time may gain pressure from remaining uninterrupted. The audience has fewer opportunities to step outside the fiction and reconsider it over a drink.

That continuity has a cost. Attention is not an unlimited resource, and an unbroken performance asks people to manage comfort, concentration and practical needs without a pause. The choice should consider the audience as carefully as the artistic shape of the piece.

An interval can be structurally expressive. A play may need a gap between two worlds, two periods or two incompatible accounts of an event. The conversation in the foyer can become part of the experience, allowing spectators to test an interpretation before returning to see it challenged.

Equally, a short work can lose momentum when divided merely because a two-part evening seems more traditional. A break creates a second beginning. The production must earn it, and the actors must find a way to re-establish the emotional weather when everyone returns.

We would not prescribe one format for London's smaller stages. A devised performance, a chamber drama and a sharply paced comedy have different rhythms. Duration alone cannot settle the question.

The interesting question is not whether a play is long enough for an interval. It is what the audience should be thinking, feeling and carrying when the lights come up — and whether the play is better served by keeping them in the dark.
