A synthetic voice still raises a human question

An actor-focused explanation of why recording consent and future voice reuse should be discussed separately.

Acting UK · 21st November 2026

Recording a performance and authorising its future synthetic reproduction are not necessarily the same decision. As voice technology becomes more capable, actors have good reason to distinguish the session they are being paid for from possible uses of the resulting material.

A contract may describe the intended production clearly while leaving later processing, training or reuse less obvious. We recommend looking for language about digital replicas, generated performances, modification, duration, territory and transfer to third parties. These terms can matter long after the microphone has been switched off.

Consent should be specific enough for a performer to understand what is being permitted. A recording used to edit a particular scene is different in character from a model capable of generating new lines in the actor's voice. The practical and reputational consequences may differ as well.

Payment is only part of the question. An actor may also want to know who can approve a new use, whether a synthetic performance will be labelled, and what happens if the original production changes ownership. The answer will depend on the agreement and the applicable law; there is no universal clause that resolves every case.

Technology can help with accessibility, localisation and production workflows. That potential does not remove the need for informed negotiation. We can welcome useful tools while insisting that the people whose performances make them possible remain part of the decision.

The sensible habit is to read the rights being granted, not merely the fee being offered. A familiar voice is a professional asset, even when the person speaking is no longer in the room.
