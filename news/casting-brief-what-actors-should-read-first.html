The casting brief is telling you more than the character's age

What a casting notice reveals about a role, and what an actor should resist reading into it.

Acting UK · 19th November 2026

A casting brief can look like a shopping list: age range, accent, a few adjectives and a deadline. Yet the most useful information is often hidden in the relationships between those details. A character described as confident but desperate to please is not a contradiction to correct. It is the beginning of a playable tension.

The first task is to separate instructions from interpretation. Submission requirements, dates, location and eligibility are practical facts. Character descriptions are invitations to make choices. An actor who confuses the two may spend hours inventing a backstory while missing the one instruction that decides whether a tape can be considered.

Age ranges are particularly easy to misunderstand. They usually describe the age a performer should plausibly play, not necessarily the number on a passport. The specific notice governs, of course, but an actor's job is to understand the screen or stage impression the production is seeking.

Adjectives deserve interrogation. 'Warm', 'difficult' and 'ambitious' are not actions. A scene becomes more interesting when the actor asks what the person wants, what stands in the way and what they do to change another person's mind. A character who is simply 'angry' has nowhere to go.

Research can help when the brief names a world, period or profession, but it should illuminate the scene rather than bury it. A small, precise observation is worth more than a borrowed collection of mannerisms. The script must remain the authority on what happens between the characters.

We recommend reading the full brief once for logistics and again for dramatic possibilities. It is a modest discipline, but it prevents an audition from becoming a response to a role the production never actually described.
