The self-tape is a performance, not a camera test

A practical guide to framing, eyelines and making an audition scene work without turning it into a miniature film.

Acting UK · 18th November 2026

The self-tape has changed the geography of an audition. The first encounter between performer and casting team may now take place through a small rectangle on a laptop screen. The temptation is to treat that rectangle as a technical problem. It is still, primarily, an acting problem.

A clean frame helps the viewer see a face and follow a thought. It does not need to look like a commercial. Unless the casting instructions say otherwise, a steady camera, an uncluttered background and sound that carries every word will do more than an elaborate lighting arrangement. The camera should not have to compete with the performance.

Eyelines matter because relationships matter. A reader placed just beside the lens can give the scene a believable partner without asking the actor to stare into the camera. The right distance depends on the brief: too wide and expression disappears; too tight and a simple movement can become a dramatic event.

The more difficult question is what to do between lines. Listening is not an interval in which the actor waits to speak. It is the work. A good tape allows a change of mind to register, even when the other character is heard only as an off-camera voice.

Preparation should begin with the actual instructions: requested scenes, framing, slate, deadline, file format and naming conventions. Those practical details are part of the professional exchange. There is little artistic virtue in an excellent scene delivered in a format the recipient cannot open.

An actor can make a handful of considered takes and choose the one in which the scene feels most alive. Repeating a scene until every accident has been removed can also remove the spontaneity that made it worth watching. We would rather see a clear choice than a technically polished absence of one.
