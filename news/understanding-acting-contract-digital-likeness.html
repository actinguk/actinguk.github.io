Before signing away a performance, read the digital clauses

Why consent, duration and future uses deserve careful attention when actors review agreements involving recorded performances.

Acting UK · 18th November 2026

A performance used to end when the camera stopped, at least in the ordinary imagination. Digital reproduction has made that assumption increasingly unreliable. A recording can be edited, repurposed or combined with technology that creates a new appearance or voice. The contract therefore deserves more attention than the reassuring phrase 'standard terms'.

Actors should distinguish permission to record an agreed performance from permission to generate or reuse a digital likeness in future material. Those are not necessarily the same activity. The precise wording, the territory, the length of the permission and the types of use covered can change what a performer is agreeing to.

A useful first reading asks simple questions. What is being captured? Who may use it? For which production and platforms? Can it be altered? Is further consent required for a materially different use? What payment or consultation, if any, is attached to that use? If the answers are not visible, the ambiguity itself is worth raising.

Industry agreements and individual contracts are not interchangeable. Collective negotiations may set minimum standards for particular productions, but an actor still needs to understand the document presented for the actual job. Rules and protections also differ by medium and engagement.

The practical advice is neither to panic about technology nor to sign in a hurry. Keep copies of the offer and correspondence, ask for unclear provisions to be explained in writing and seek appropriate union or qualified legal guidance before accepting consequential rights clauses.

New tools can be useful to productions. The essential question is whether the people whose work gives those tools value remain informed participants. Consent is not a decorative box at the bottom of a form; it is the substance of the arrangement.

This is an original Acting UK editorial essay, not a report of a newly announced production or a first-hand performance review.
