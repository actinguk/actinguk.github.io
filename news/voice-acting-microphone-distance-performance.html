The microphone is not a substitute for listening

Why voice performance depends on intention, distance and a believable relationship beyond the recording booth.

Acting UK · 20th November 2026

A microphone can capture a whisper with astonishing intimacy. It cannot, on its own, give that whisper a reason to exist. Voice acting begins with the same questions as any other performance: who is speaking, to whom, and what must change by the end of the line?

The recording environment changes the scale of the work. In a theatre, a performer may need to project a thought across a large room. In a booth, a slight change of breath can become conspicuous. The actor must be technically aware without allowing technical awareness to become the performance.

Distance from the microphone affects level, proximity and the likelihood of unwanted noise. A recording engineer or director may specify a position, and that instruction should take priority over any universal rule. A consistent working distance gives the recording team usable material; a sudden dramatic lean can make editing unnecessarily difficult.

Yet physical restraint does not mean emotional stillness. A voice performance can contain urgency, hesitation, resistance and humour without requiring the actor to imitate the surface sound of those feelings. An intention is generally more useful than an instruction to sound sad.

Scenes recorded separately from other performers require imaginative listening. The actor may hear a guide track, a director reading opposite them or nothing but a cue. The task is to make the absent partner specific. A response that genuinely follows another person's thought has more life than a perfectly timed line delivered into a vacuum.

Scripts for animation, games, drama and commercial work place different demands on rhythm and consistency. The brief matters, as do pronunciation notes, pickups and the need to reproduce a performance later. Flexibility is part of the craft, not an optional courtesy.

We think the best voice work makes the technology disappear. The listener should hear a person pursuing something, not an actor demonstrating what a microphone can record.

Good technique protects the performance. It does not replace it.
